01 / Formulation Design

The product we are developing.

Our proposed product is a 100 mg/mL pyrazinamide (PZA) oral suspension designed for paediatric tuberculosis treatment. The formulation uses a defined excipient system to address suspension behaviour, palatability, wetting, preservation and pH control.

FORMULATION DEVELOPMENT

Alternative Materials Considered During Development.

Several pharmaceutical materials could perform similar functions within an oral suspension. Alternatives were considered based on suspension stability, palatability, compatibility, safety and suitability for paediatric use.

01

Suspending Agent

Selected: Sodium carboxymethylcellulose (NaCMC)
Alternative: Xanthan gum or hydroxypropyl methylcellulose (HPMC)

These polymers can increase vehicle viscosity and help maintain the dispersed drug particles. NaCMC was selected as the proposed starting material because of its established use as a suspending and viscosity-enhancing agent.

02

Wetting Agent

Selected: Polysorbate 80
Alternative: Polysorbate 20

A wetting agent helps the aqueous vehicle contact the hydrophobic drug particles and improves their dispersion. Polysorbate 20 could be investigated as an alternative, but the final choice would depend on compatibility, dispersion performance and stability testing.

03

Sweetener

Selected: Sucrose
Alternative: Sorbitol

Sucrose was selected primarily to improve palatability. Sorbitol could provide an alternative sweetening approach, particularly where a lower-sugar formulation is desirable. Its suitability would need to be assessed for taste, gastrointestinal tolerance and overall formulation performance.

04

Preservative

Selected: Potassium sorbate
Alternative: Sodium benzoate

An aqueous suspension may require protection against microbial growth. Sodium benzoate could be investigated as an alternative preservative, although preservative effectiveness depends on factors such as pH, concentration and interactions with other formulation components.

05

Buffer System

Selected: Citrate-based system
Alternative: Phosphate-based system

A buffer system can help maintain the formulation within an appropriate pH range. A phosphate-based system could be evaluated as an alternative, but the final buffer must be selected according to drug stability, preservative effectiveness and excipient compatibility.

06

Flavour

Selected: Paediatric-compatible fruit flavour
Alternative: Another age-appropriate fruit flavour

Flavour selection is important because tuberculosis treatment involves repeated administration. Alternative fruit flavours could be screened according to taste acceptability, compatibility with the formulation and preference testing in the intended patient population.

DEVELOPMENT PRINCIPLE

An alternative is not automatically a better material.

Any replacement material would need to be evaluated for pharmaceutical performance, excipient compatibility, paediatric suitability, palatability, physical stability, chemical stability and microbial stability before being selected for the final formulation.

FORMULATION DEVELOPMENT

Sustainability Considered Throughout Development.

Sustainability was considered alongside pharmaceutical performance, paediatric suitability and product stability. The aim is not simply to select materials that appear environmentally friendly, but to reduce environmental impact without compromising product quality or patient safety.

01

Cellulose-Derived Polymer

Sodium carboxymethylcellulose (NaCMC)

Sodium carboxymethylcellulose (NaCMC) is a cellulose-derived polymer used to provide viscosity and help maintain the drug in suspension. Cellulose-based materials can offer renewable feedstock advantages, but environmental biodegradability depends on the specific chemical derivative and should not be assumed automatically.

Renewable feedstock potential
02

Palatability Material

Sucrose

Sucrose is a plant-derived sweetener that can improve palatability and therefore support acceptability of a repeatedly administered paediatric medicine. The amount used should be optimised so that sufficient taste improvement is achieved without unnecessary excipient use.

Plant-derived material
03

Preservative Selection

Potassium sorbate

Potassium sorbate provides microbial protection in the aqueous formulation. Environmental fate is also relevant when selecting a preservative, since unnecessary environmental persistence should be avoided. The preservative concentration should therefore be kept as low as scientifically justified while maintaining adequate microbial protection.

Minimise unnecessary material use
04

Efficient Excipient Use

Functional materials at optimised levels

Sustainability also depends on how much material is used. Suspending agents, wetting agents, preservatives and flavouring should be optimised to the lowest effective levels that still provide the required pharmaceutical performance. This can reduce unnecessary raw-material consumption and formulation waste.

Optimise quantities
05

Packaging

Protect the product while reducing waste

Packaging must protect the suspension from leakage, contamination and environmental conditions during storage and transport. At the same time, packaging material should be minimised where possible and recyclable or recoverable materials should be considered where they provide adequate pharmaceutical protection.

Reduce packaging waste
06

Manufacturing & Transport

Reduce avoidable resource use

Manufacturing and distribution can contribute to the environmental footprint of the product through energy use, water consumption, raw-material transport and packaging. Efficient batch processing, appropriate sourcing and avoiding unnecessary transport distances could reduce the overall footprint without compromising product quality.

Resource efficiency
BALANCING SUSTAINABILITY & QUALITY

The most sustainable material is not useful if it cannot perform its pharmaceutical function.

Final material selection would therefore require a balance between environmental considerations and pharmaceutical requirements. Any proposed change would need to maintain dose uniformity, physical stability, chemical stability, microbial quality, palatability and paediatric suitability before being adopted.

DEVELOPMENT APPROACH

Sustainability is built into the formulation, not added afterwards.

01

Select materials according to both pharmaceutical function and environmental considerations.

02

03

Minimise manufacturing and packaging waste where product quality allows.

04

03 / Manufacturing

Building the suspension.

The order of addition is designed to support proper hydration, wetting, dispersion and uniformity of the final suspension.

01

Prepare the workspace

Clean and sanitise the manufacturing area and equipment before beginning the formulation process.

02

Hydrate the NaCMC

Disperse the calculated sodium carboxymethylcellulose (NaCMC) into a portion of purified water and allow adequate hydration.

03

Prepare the aqueous phase

Prepare the vehicle containing sucrose, potassium sorbate and the citrate buffer components.

04

Wet the PZA

Accurately weigh the pyrazinamide (PZA). Wet the powder using a small amount of wetting-agent-containing vehicle until a smooth paste is formed.

05

Geometric dilution

Gradually incorporate the vehicle into the PZA paste using geometric dilution, mixing thoroughly after each addition.

06

Make up to volume

Transfer to a calibrated vessel and add purified water q.s. to 100 mL. Add the selected flavour at its predetermined concentration and mix thoroughly.

07

Fill and package

Transfer the finished suspension into a suitable light-resistant multidose bottle and supply a calibrated oral syringe.

08

Quality control

The finished formulation proceeds to physical, chemical and microbiological quality assessments.

04 / Suspension Science

What happens inside the bottle?

PZA is dispersed as solid particles rather than being completely dissolved in the vehicle. During storage, gravity can cause particles to settle.

This makes redispersibility important. The suspension should be capable of being adequately redistributed before administration.

The formulation therefore uses a suspending agent and wetting agent as part of the strategy for controlling suspension behaviour.

01 — Sedimentation
Particles settle during storage.
02 — Shake
The bottle is shaken.
03 — Redisperse
More uniform distribution for dosing.

Packaging and administration are part of formulation development because the product must remain protected while allowing accurate paediatric dosing.

06 / TRANSPORT CONSIDERATIONS

Protecting product quality during transport.

Because the product is a multidose oral suspension, transport conditions must protect the formulation and packaging from physical and environmental stresses that could affect product quality before administration.

Secure packaging

The suspension should be transported in a securely closed, leak-resistant multidose container to minimise the risk of leakage, contamination or loss of product.

Temperature protection

The product should be protected from excessive heat and freezing during transport. Temperature exposure should remain within the conditions supported by formulation and stability studies.

Light protection

The container should be protected from direct sunlight and excessive light exposure during transport to help maintain the quality of the formulation.

Handling and movement

Excessive or unnecessary agitation should be minimised during transport to reduce foaming and physical stress on the suspension. The formulation should nevertheless remain readily redispersible by shaking before administration.

Key consideration

Transport conditions should preserve the physical integrity, chemical quality and usability of the suspension from manufacture through to administration.

DEVELOPMENT CONSIDERATIONS

Unforeseen Challenges That May Arise During Development.

Developing a paediatric oral suspension involves challenges that may only become apparent during formulation, testing, storage or patient use. These risks would need to be investigated and addressed before the product could be considered suitable for routine use.

01

Sedimentation & Redispersibility

Drug particles may settle during storage. If the sediment becomes too compact, the suspension may be difficult to redisperse before administration. The formulation would therefore need to provide acceptable physical stability while remaining easy to shake and redisperse.

Development response: Evaluate sedimentation behaviour, redispersibility and physical stability during formulation testing.
02

Dose Uniformity

A suspension must deliver a consistent amount of pyrazinamide (PZA) in each administered dose. Poor redispersion, inadequate shaking or particle settling could potentially result in doses that do not contain the intended amount of drug.

Development response: Assess dose uniformity throughout the container and provide clear instructions for shaking before use.
03

Chemical Stability

The active pharmaceutical ingredient (API) and excipients must remain chemically stable throughout the intended shelf life. Changes in pH, temperature or interactions between formulation components could affect product quality over time.

Development response: Conduct stability studies to monitor drug content, degradation and relevant formulation properties.
04

Microbial Contamination

Because the product is an aqueous suspension, microbial contamination is an important consideration. Repeated opening of the container during treatment may increase opportunities for contamination if the product is not adequately protected and handled.

Development response: Evaluate preservative effectiveness, microbial quality and appropriate handling and storage conditions.
05

Palatability & Adherence

Pyrazinamide (PZA) has an unpleasant taste, making palatability particularly important in a paediatric formulation. However, increasing sweetener or flavour levels does not automatically produce an acceptable product and may introduce other formulation limitations.

Development response: Optimise flavour and sweetening while assessing acceptability, stability and overall formulation performance.
06

Packaging & Patient Handling

The product may be exposed to leakage, temperature fluctuations, incorrect storage or inaccurate dose measurement during real-world use. The packaging and dosing system must therefore be practical for caregivers while protecting product quality.

Development response: Assess container compatibility, closure integrity, dosing accuracy and handling instructions.
DEVELOPMENT PRINCIPLE

Challenges must be tested, not assumed away.

Each potential challenge would be investigated through formulation optimisation, physical and chemical testing, stability studies and appropriate quality-control assessments. This ensures that the final formulation is supported by evidence rather than assumptions.
